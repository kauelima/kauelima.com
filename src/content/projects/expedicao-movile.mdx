**Delivery Center**, at the time one of Movile's companies, ran a dispatch
floor fed by several kitchens at once. Orders had to be collected from each
kitchen, staged, and handed to the right driver — and every one of those
handoffs was a place the order could go quiet.

## The problem

The operation had two halves that were being managed as if they were one:
**pickup** from the kitchens, and **dispatch** to the drivers. Between them sat
a staging area where an order was physically present but informationally
invisible.

That produced the usual failure modes of a busy floor:

- **No live position.** An order was "somewhere between the kitchen and the
  bike", which is not a state anyone can act on.
- **Handoffs left no trace.** When an order went missing, there was no record
  of who last held it.
- **The floor is a bad reading environment.** Loud, fast, and no one is going
  to stop and parse a table.

## What I did

The app was designed around a single idea: **every change of custody is a
scan**, and the scan is what makes the operation legible.

**One primitive, used everywhere.** QR scanning covers kitchen pickup, staging,
driver assignment and handoff. There is one gesture to learn, and each scan
writes the timestamp and the holder, so the order's history assembles itself
from work people were already doing.

**Colour carries the state, not text.** Scan screens go full-bleed green,
red or neutral for confirmed, rejected and pending. On a dispatch floor the
answer has to survive being read at arm's length, in motion, in a second — so
the state is the whole screen rather than a label on it.

**The staging area got addresses.** Short slot codes (A1, D1) turn "put it down
somewhere" into a location the next person can be sent to, which is what closes
the gap between kitchen and driver.

**Two roles, one model.** Operator and driver flows share the same underlying
object and the same scan, so the app is a single account of the order rather
than two systems reconciling after the fact.
