Jam is a shared family calendar built on a simple observation: the hard part of
family logistics is not *displaying* the schedule, it is **getting the schedule
in there at all**. Practices, half-days, birthday parties and permission slips
arrive as email, screenshots and group-chat messages — and one parent silently
absorbs the job of transcribing them.

Jam was founded by Jessica Etting and Amanda Roessler, both parents, and went
through Techstars. It ships on iOS, Android and web, and has been covered by
Mashable, Fast Company, PureWow and Scary Mommy.

## The problem

A family calendar is a coordination tool disguised as a display tool. Existing
options optimised the wrong half:

- **Capture is manual.** Every event has to be read, interpreted and re-typed.
- **Load is invisible.** The parent doing the organising gets no credit and no relief.
- **Context is scattered.** The event is in the calendar; the address, the
  what-to-bring and the reply-by date are back in the original email.

## The product

Three pillars, in the order a family actually experiences them.

### Organization

One place for all the family things — every member's events, schedules and
responsibilities in a single view, rather than one calendar per parent plus a
paper copy on the fridge.

### Coordination

Shared to-do lists and assignable tasks, so responsibility can be handed to a
specific person instead of implicitly landing on whoever noticed first. This is
the feature that makes the invisible load visible.

### Automation

Forward an email — a school newsletter, a party invitation, a season schedule —
and Jam extracts the events and adds them to the calendar. It turns the most
tedious step into a single gesture, and it is the reason the calendar stays
current instead of decaying after week three.

## Approach

The core bet was that **capture, not display, is the retention lever**. A family
calendar that requires manual entry degrades predictably: heavy use in week one,
abandonment by week four, because the cost of keeping it accurate exceeds the
benefit of it being accurate. Email forwarding attacks that directly — a
deliberately low-ceremony interface with no integration to configure, no
permissions to grant, and no new habit beyond one the user already has.

That bet set the design constraints too. The interface had to work for a parent
standing in a school pickup line: one hand, ten seconds, high stakes. Which
drove strong colour-coding per family member, generous touch targets, and an
information hierarchy that answers *whose, when, where* before anything else.

Cross-platform parity mattered more than platform idiom — families mix iPhone
and Android, and a feature that behaves differently by device creates exactly
the coordination failure the product exists to remove.
